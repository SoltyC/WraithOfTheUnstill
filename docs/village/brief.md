# Meadow Village — Environment and Architectural Design Brief

## Purpose

Design and build a large, cohesive medieval-fantasy village in a meadow biome. This document covers **environment design, spatial layout, architecture, terrain, stairs, paths, materials, and visual composition only**.

The central feature is a **large hill rising from the middle of the village**, with an extensive monumental stairway leading to a **huge manor at the summit**. The manor is the final architectural focal point of the settlement. The existing project already defines the boss fight, so do not redesign, replace, or add mechanics to the boss encounter. Focus only on the physical environment around it.

## 1. Overall design direction

Create a settlement that feels old, lived-in, and organically developed around the central hill.

- Use a medieval European-inspired architectural language: timber-framed cottages, pale plaster, local stone, steep roofs, slate or shingle, and selective thatch.
- The village should feel large and dense near the foot of the hill, then become more open towards farms and meadow at the edges.
- Avoid a rigid grid, perfectly circular rings, repeated identical houses, and empty decorative space.
- Roads and building plots should adapt to the contours of the terrain.
- Use the manor and stairway as the main visual landmarks, visible from several approaches through gaps between rooftops.
- Keep the meadow biome legible: rolling grass, wildflowers, hedgerows, scattered mature trees, farm plots, and distant low hills.

Treat the dimensions below as starting proportions, adapting them to the project's existing world scale, navigation, and camera.

## 2. Settlement layout

Build the village in several connected layers:

1. **Outer meadow:** Open grassland, irregular farm plots, orchards, fences, footpaths, and a few outlying cottages.
2. **Village edge:** Looser residential plots, barns, sheds, small gardens, and cart tracks.
3. **Lower village:** A dense but irregular network of streets, cottages, shops, workshops, courtyards, and small public spaces.
4. **Hill base:** A clear transition from ordinary village streets to the broad lower stair approach. Leave enough open space for the hill to feel imposing.
5. **Central hill:** A substantial natural-looking rise occupying the centre of the settlement. The slopes should be terraced and supported where needed by old retaining walls, but should remain partly covered in grass, shrubs, and mature trees.
6. **Upper approach:** Monumental stairs climb the hill in long visible runs, interrupted by landings, turns, terraces, and gates.
7. **Summit manor:** A huge manor complex on the hilltop, with a forecourt and a clear, dramatic silhouette above the rooftops.

The hill should not be a small mound with a building placed on it. It must be a defining landform that shapes the roads, buildings, sightlines, and overall village plan.

## 3. Central hill

Make the hill the dominant feature of the village.

- Give it a broad footprint and a high summit, with slopes visible from the main entrance and market area.
- Use an irregular oval or asymmetrical form rather than a perfect cone.
- Shape the lower slopes with a combination of natural grassy banks, exposed rock, terraced gardens, stone retaining walls, and scattered trees.
- Keep the upper slopes more architectural and controlled, with retaining walls and carefully placed terraces supporting the monumental staircase.
- Integrate the hill into the surrounding terrain rather than making it look like a separate artificial platform.
- Avoid covering every surface with buildings; the open slope and stairway need visual breathing room.
- Make sure the manor roofline and upper stair sections can be glimpsed from important streets and open spaces.

The hill should feel old and established, as if the village developed around it over generations.

## 4. Monumental stairway

The climb to the manor is the central architectural experience. It should feel long, imposing, and physically integrated into the hill, not like a single straight staircase pasted onto a slope.

### Overall composition

- Create a very long ascent from the hill's base to the manor forecourt.
- Use several substantial flights of stone stairs separated by broad landings and terraces.
- Let the stair route bend or switch back along the hillside where appropriate, but preserve strong views of the upper stairway and manor.
- Use a strong central axis for at least part of the ascent so the manor feels like the destination.
- Make the stairway readable from below, with the upper sections appearing above retaining walls, trees, and terraces.
- Keep the route wide enough to feel ceremonial and monumental. It should be considerably wider than ordinary village steps.
- Integrate smaller side stairs and paths into the slopes, but make the main route visually unmistakable.

### Stair flights and landings

Vary the ascent through a sequence of architectural spaces:

- **Lower approach:** A broad paved path or shallow first flight leading from the village streets to the base of the hill.
- **First major flight:** Wide stone steps flanked by substantial retaining walls, low parapets, or balustrades.
- **Intermediate terrace:** A generous landing with a view back over the rooftops. Use stone paving, a few benches or planters, and restrained architectural detailing.
- **Second major flight:** A longer or steeper run that makes the climb feel more imposing. It may turn along the hill to reveal a new view.
- **Upper terrace:** A more formal space with stronger symmetry, heavier stonework, and a clear view of the manor.
- **Final ascent:** A grand, direct stair flight leading through a gate, between towers, or along a formal approach into the manor forecourt.

Use the existing navigation and movement conventions of the game when choosing step height, tread depth, collision, and slope transitions. The staircase must be comfortable and reliable to traverse in gameplay, even while looking monumental.

### Stair construction and details

- Use worn limestone or pale grey stone with darker seams and weathering.
- Vary stone colour slightly rather than using a uniform flat texture.
- Add chipped corners, subtle erosion, moss in shaded joints, and grass growing in selected cracks.
- Use low stone parapets on exposed edges and substantial retaining walls where the hill rises beside the stairs.
- Add occasional stone lanterns, iron sconces, carved posts, or simple statues, increasing in formality closer to the summit.
- Keep decoration sparse enough that the scale of the stairs remains the main impression.
- Avoid repetitive copy-pasted landings or identical decorative objects at every interval.

## 5. The manor at the summit

The manor should be enormous compared with ordinary village buildings and clearly visible from the lower settlement. It should feel like an old seat of power built on a commanding hilltop.

The project already establishes the boss fight. **Do not change the boss, encounter design, combat mechanics, or fight progression.** Build the manor exterior and surrounding architecture to support the existing encounter.

### Building massing

- Create a large central hall as the dominant volume, with several connected wings.
- Use a combination of tall stone walls, timber-framed upper sections where appropriate, steep roofs, gables, chimneys, and towers.
- Use asymmetrical massing with a deliberate main axis facing the staircase.
- Include towers or tall roof features at selected corners to establish a strong skyline without making the manor resemble an enormous castle.
- Make the manor footprint large enough to dominate the summit but leave room for a formal forecourt and visible terrace edges.
- Ensure the manor is not hidden behind trees, walls, or roof clutter when seen from the village below.

### Facades and architectural hierarchy

- The front facade facing the stairs should be the most formal and imposing.
- Use a large entrance portal, heavy doors, stone framing, and tall windows.
- Use smaller windows, service doors, and simpler detailing on secondary facades.
- Add buttresses, carved stone details, weathered masonry, ivy in limited areas, and roof variations.
- Make the architecture look aged and maintained rather than uniformly pristine or entirely ruined.
- Use warm interior light in a few windows to contrast with the cool exterior stone, where appropriate for the game's lighting style.

### Forecourt and summit platform

- Place a large stone-paved forecourt between the final stair flight and the manor entrance.
- Use a restrained arrangement of paving bands, low walls, planters, statues, or stone posts to reinforce the central approach.
- Allow the terrain to fall away beyond the forecourt so the manor feels elevated above the settlement.
- Add terraces and retaining walls along the summit edge, with clear sightlines down the stairway.
- Ensure the forecourt, entrance, and manor exterior connect cleanly to the existing playable boss area without changing its mechanics or layout requirements.

## 6. Village architecture

Use a consistent material palette while varying building size and shape.

### Ordinary houses

- Mostly one-and-a-half or two storeys.
- Cream or pale beige plaster between dark timber beams.
- Steep thatched, shingled, or slate roofs.
- Small windows, shutters, heavy timber doors, and stone foundations.
- Some projecting upper floors, dormers, lean-tos, and rear extensions.
- Irregular spacing and varied roof heights.

### Shops and larger town buildings

- Two storeys, occasionally with an attic level.
- Narrow facades facing the street.
- Covered entrances, timber awnings, hanging signs, and broad shop windows where suitable.
- Stone or timber-framed ground floors with more elaborate upper facades.
- Buildings should follow the street line unevenly rather than forming a perfectly straight row.

### Workshops and agricultural buildings

- Larger footprints and simpler forms.
- Exposed timber frames, plank walls, stone bases, and broad pitched roofs.
- Large double doors, open-sided work areas, loading yards, woodpiles, and storage lean-tos.
- Locate noisy or smoky structures away from the densest residential streets where possible.

### Public and civic buildings

Use stone more heavily for the chapel, meeting hall, bridge structures, and other major landmarks. These buildings should be easy to recognise from their massing and materials rather than relying only on signage.

## 7. Streets, paths, and circulation

- Create one principal road entering from the meadow and leading towards the village centre.
- Let secondary streets branch irregularly from it, connecting the residential areas, workshops, farms, and hill base.
- Avoid a perfect ring road around the hill. Instead, use partial curved streets and several cross-connections that follow the slope.
- Keep the main road broad enough for carts and passing NPCs.
- Use narrower lanes between houses and footpaths along garden edges and the hill.
- Add small paved courts and junction widenings where streets meet.
- Use cobbles or irregular stone paving in the central village, compacted earth on secondary roads, and grass tracks at the outskirts.
- Make road surfaces change gradually at district boundaries.
- Ensure the stairway's lower entrance is easy to identify from the main streets, without making every road point directly at it.
- Keep routes around the hill connected so the landform feels part of the village rather than blocking it off completely.

## 8. Retaining walls, terraces, and hillside structures

The hill requires convincing transitions between natural terrain and built structures.

- Use retaining walls to support the upper stairway and formal terraces.
- Vary wall height according to the slope rather than making a uniform circular wall.
- Build walls from rough local stone at lower levels and more finely dressed stone near the manor.
- Include buttresses, drainage openings, coping stones, and occasional stair connections.
- Allow some walls to be partially covered by ivy or grass.
- Create small terraces with shrubs, flowering plants, and a few trees, but avoid vegetation that obscures the main stair route.
- Where streets pass close to the hill, use short retaining walls, raised garden plots, or buildings built against the slope.
- Avoid floating walls, unsupported platforms, abrupt terrain seams, and visibly artificial cut-off edges.

## 9. Landscaping and meadow integration

The village should blend into the meadow rather than end at a hard boundary.

- Use rolling terrain, irregular patches of wildflowers, tall grass, and scattered mature trees.
- Concentrate trimmed gardens and planted beds near houses and the manor.
- Use hedgerows, wooden fences, low stone walls, and drainage ditches to divide farms and gardens.
- Place small orchards and vegetable plots between residential buildings and the outer fields.
- Keep some open grassy slopes on the central hill so its scale remains visible.
- Use trees to frame views, not block the manor or the monumental staircase.
- Add worn footpaths across grass where shortcuts are plausible.
- Vary ground texture and plant density to avoid a uniform green carpet.
- Make the outer meadow more natural and less managed than the village centre.

## 10. Materials and weathering

Use a restrained, consistent palette:

- **Walls:** Warm cream plaster, pale limestone, grey-beige rubble stone.
- **Timber:** Dark weathered oak and medium brown planks.
- **Roofs:** Muted brown thatch, dark timber shingles, slate grey.
- **Roads:** Warm grey cobbles, uneven flagstones, packed earth.
- **Metal:** Dark iron fittings, hinges, sconces, and railings.
- **Vegetation:** Meadow greens, sage, muted yellow grasses, and restrained wildflower colours.

Add weathering according to exposure and building importance. The lower village should show wear around doorways and road edges. The hill stairs should have erosion and moss in sheltered joints. The manor should be more carefully maintained, but still show age through stone variation, weathered roof edges, and subtle discoloration.

## 11. Composition and visibility

The hill, staircase, and manor should control the visual composition of the entire village.

- Establish a clear view of the manor from the southern approach or another major entrance.
- Reveal more of the stairway as the player moves through the village.
- Use roof gaps, street bends, small squares, and changes in elevation to create successive views of the hill.
- Keep the manor's upper silhouette visible above most surrounding buildings.
- Use the staircase as a strong visual line, broken into sections by landings and terraces.
- Avoid placing tall trees or oversized buildings directly in front of key stair sections.
- From the summit, provide a clear view over the village roofs and surrounding meadow.
- Keep visual clutter low around the hill base and the stairway entrance so the ascent is immediately legible.

## 12. Implementation order

Build in this order to maintain consistent scale and avoid reworking the terrain:

1. Block out the meadow terrain and village boundary.
2. Establish the central hill and summit height.
3. Block out the manor footprint, forecourt, and summit terraces.
4. Build the main stair route, including all flights, turns, landings, and retaining walls.
5. Lay out the main roads and the hill-base approach.
6. Place the market and major public buildings.
7. Fill residential streets with varied house footprints.
8. Add workshops, farmsteads, outbuildings, and boundary walls.
9. Integrate the river and bridge if the surrounding world layout supports them.
10. Add terrain blending, gardens, meadow vegetation, fences, props, and surface materials.
11. Check navigation, collision, step traversal, camera sightlines, and performance.
12. Refine silhouettes, roof variation, lighting, and weathering.

## 13. Quality checklist

Before considering the environment complete, verify that:

- [ ] The central hill is large and prominent enough to define the village.
- [ ] The manor is enormous relative to the surrounding buildings and clearly visible from below.
- [ ] The staircase is long, monumental, and made of several distinct flights and landings.
- [ ] The stairs are traversable using the game's existing movement and collision systems.
- [ ] Retaining walls and terraces follow the hill's contours convincingly.
- [ ] The village layout is organic rather than grid-based or perfectly circular.
- [ ] Buildings have varied footprints, rooflines, heights, and facades.
- [ ] Roads connect naturally and leave enough space around important landmarks.
- [ ] The architecture transitions gradually into farms and meadow.
- [ ] The hill, stairway, and manor remain the dominant visual composition.
- [ ] There are no floating structures, abrupt terrain seams, repetitive building clusters, or blocked critical sightlines.
- [ ] The existing boss fight remains unchanged; this brief only specifies the surrounding environment and architectural setting.

## Final instruction

Prioritise **coherent spatial design, convincing medieval architecture, a monumental central hill, and an exceptionally long stone staircase leading to a huge summit manor**. Establish terrain and building massing before decorating. Do not add quests, NPC storylines, new combat mechanics, or boss-fight changes. Adapt dimensions to the existing game world and preserve its established gameplay systems.
